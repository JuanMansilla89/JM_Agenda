# Dynamic Modeling of a Coarse Ore Stockpile for a Mine-to-Mill Digital Twin

## Scope and evidence base

This report synthesizes recent academic and industrial evidence on coarse ore stockpile modeling, with emphasis on public-domain work from *Minerals Engineering*, *Powder Technology*, *Minerals*, and adjacent digital-twin, RTD, and bulk-solids literature. The strongest recent public evidence base sits around dynamic residence-time modeling, 3D cellular-automata stockpile simulation, DEM sensitivity studies, FMS/survey-driven spatial models, and generalized digital-twin architectures. Publicly available implementations for large conveyor-fed copper coarse ore stockpiles remain materially thinner than the broader literature on ROM stockpiles, blending yards, silos, and grinding-circuit RTD, so some operating thresholds below are explicitly framed as engineering acceptance targets rather than published universal standards. citeturn25search0turn5view0turn3view2turn33view0turn11view3

From an implementation perspective, your proposed two-layer architecture is well aligned with the literature: a CPU-light dynamic mass-balance / RTD core for continuous operation, and a spatially discretized recalibration layer tied to survey updates. That architecture is consistent with the way modern digital twins in mining are described: real-time data assimilation, physically meaningful internal states, and an on-demand spatial layer that is periodically reconciled to measurements. citeturn11view3turn11view1turn3view2

## State of the art and operating implications

The industry has historically started from low-resolution stockpile representations such as averaged grades, fixed delays, arithmetic blending, or queue-like bookkeeping. Recent literature argues that these approximations become inadequate once the objective shifts from inventory accounting to dynamic mine-to-mill forecasting, especially when reclaim geometry, segregation, and mode changes matter. In the Aitik “virtual silo” case, transportation time based on mean capacities could be as much as 50% lower than time calculated from dynamic momentary values, which is a strong argument against shift-average delay assumptions for your use case. Similarly, Young and Rogers show that stockpile averaging to a single grade is too imprecise for modern digital mine-to-mill control. citeturn24search0turn33view0

A fit-for-purpose comparison of modeling families is set out below.

| Approach | Representation logic | Strategic strengths | Structural limitations | Best role in your architecture |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical FIFO / fixed-lag bookkeeping | Material leaves in entry order or after a fixed delay | Extremely auditable; near-zero compute cost | Does not represent mixing, ratholing, feeder asymmetry, or mode changes; weak under low-level starvation | Only as a baseline comparator and sanity-check |
| Parametric RTD | Convolution kernel between inflow and reclaim | Best cost/benefit for quasi-real-time CPU execution; natural fit for micro-batches | Needs regime-dependent calibration; cannot localize geometry by itself | Recommended backbone for the **Light Model** |
| Tanks-in-series / generalized n-CSTR | RTD expressed as a cascade of mixed cells | Physically interpretable mean/variance; compact parameter set; can represent imperfect mixing and bypassing | Still lumped; spatial fidelity is limited | Best parametric basis for RTD in the **Light Model** |
| Discretized spatial / CA model | Stockpile volume divided into cells with tracked properties | Captures stacking pattern, reclaim zones, segregation, surface evolution, feeder-specific draw | More calibration effort; higher computational burden; stronger data dependency | Recommended basis for the **Spatial Model** |
| Simplified DEM | Particle-scale mechanics with reduced fidelity vs full DEM | Best for mechanism discovery and offline sensitivity analysis | Usually too computationally expensive for real-time plant deployment | Offline design, sensitivity studies, parameter priors |
| Machine learning | Data-driven mapping from process states to quality/PSD outputs | Strong as a soft sensor; good for regime detection and residual correction | Weak physical traceability if used alone; needs long clean history | Overlay model, not the primary source of truth |

This ranking is directly consistent with the literature. RTD is the preferred light dynamic formalism for plant-wide blending analysis and response-to-disturbance tracking; generalized tanks-in-series models connect the exponential CSTR limit, imperfect back-mixing, and plug-flow behavior with a compact parameterization; 3D CA stockpile models have now been published with industrial validation; DEM studies remain highly informative for mechanism sensitivity; and current ML work is strongest as a predictive layer on top of feeder/stockpile/process data rather than as a standalone ore-tracking engine. citeturn25search0turn30view0turn5view0turn3view6turn26search1turn26search2

For RTD parameterization, the most defensible starting point is **not** a pure exponential. The generalized n-CSTR / tanks-in-series family is the best-supported compact representation because it gives a physically interpretable shape parameter, reduces to the exponential for ideal CSTR behavior, sharpens toward plug flow as the number of tanks increases, and can even represent bypassing/short-circuiting for \(n<1\) in the generalized form. In other words, gamma-like RTDs grounded in tanks-in-series theory are the most operationally credible first choice for a coarse ore stockpile light model. A delayed or shifted kernel is usually required as well. citeturn30view0turn29search3

A practical implication for your design is that the RTD should be **mode-conditioned**, not globally constant. The broader RTD literature shows that flow and mixing behavior depend on geometry, bulk properties, and flow rate; the solids-handling literature shows that funnel flow, outlet configuration, wall friction, and segregation are all first-order determinants of RTD shape; and the Aitik case shows that dynamic momentary flow conditions matter materially. For your stockpile, the minimum regime split should be by stockpile level band, reclaim pattern, and tractor-assisted versus gravity-only operation. citeturn32search7turn3view7turn24search0turn32search15

On the specific question of **live stock versus dead stock**, the literature is unusually clear. Zamorano defines live capacity as material stored and retrieved without auxiliary equipment, and dead capacity as material requiring auxiliary equipment such as bulldozers or front-end loaders. He further notes that on large stockpiles it is difficult to obtain live capacities greater than 20–25% of total volume, whereas smaller stockpiles can achieve up to roughly one-third. Morrell and Valery note that for a simple conical stockpile with a central offtake, live capacity can be only about 25% of total volume. Additional bulk-solids work shows that adjacent feeder interaction and feeder arrangement can materially improve reclaimability and live capacity. citeturn16view0turn14search14turn15search0turn15search4

That matters directly for Las Bambas. Your operating symptom—SAG 2 losing natural gravity feed below about 60% level and requiring tractor push for roughly 20% of operating time—is precisely the kind of phenomenon that a single homogeneous inventory state cannot explain. In modeling terms, the stockpile should be partitioned into at least three reclaim states: **gravity-live stock**, **conditional stock that becomes reclaimable only under tractor assistance**, and **structurally dead / low-mobility stock**. The exact fractions are site-specific and must be calibrated, but the public literature strongly supports the principle that a large funnel-flow coarse ore stockpile contains a large non-live fraction unless feeder geometry and flow promotion are exceptionally favorable. citeturn16view0turn17view1turn15search0

The public state of the art for **spatially resolved stockpile models** is now mature enough to justify your proposed Spatial Model. The JKMRC 3D cellular automata work models stockpiles as continuous feeding and discharging 3D grids with tracked cell properties, includes size segregation mechanisms, and reports industrial validation of stockpile height and product size over two months of operation. Earlier JKMRC outputs also report validation against industrial data from two mine sites and explicitly position the model as a soft sensor and process-optimization layer. In parallel, the 2021 *Minerals* paper by Young and Rogers shows an operationally workable spatial model for a large stockpile using FMS data, bench domains, and geostatistical interpolation, while explicitly highlighting the need to account for dozer movement and survey-based trimming. CSIRO’s operational stockpile work similarly couples 3D mapping, modeling, reconciliation, and even bulldozer scheduling. citeturn5view0turn6search8turn10search5turn10search6turn33view0turn34view1turn11view1

The digital-twin literature supports an even broader framing. In the 2021 *Minerals* paper on distributed particle simulation, pseudo-particles carry position, mass, size distribution, mineral concentrations, and mechanical properties through a network of virtual assets. That is conceptually very close to your micro-batch tracking in the Light Model and your cell-based property propagation in the Spatial Model. The principal implementation insight is that material tracking becomes far more robust when the twin explicitly stores material identity and not only aggregate flow. citeturn3view2

The literature also supports your concern about **stacking pattern changes** and **asymmetric reclaim geometry**. The JKMRC stockpile work explicitly notes that typical coarse ore stockpiles are fed by fixed or moving conveyors and that dynamic feed trajectory matters; the Powder Technology RFID-sensitivity study found that wall friction, feed-belt speed, segregation in the flow region, and charging methods significantly affect internal blending; and bulk-solids reclaim literature shows that feeder number, spacing, and position directly govern whether flow channels intersect and destabilize ratholes. In other words, when the plant changes discharge point or discharge direction by level, that is not bookkeeping detail; it is a first-order model mode change. citeturn15search2turn3view6turn17view1turn15search0

On your specific operating edge cases, the literature and industrial practice converge on several points. Tractor or dozer assistance is treated as **auxiliary reclaim**, not as normal gravity flow, and must therefore be modeled as a distinct reclaim mechanism. Dozers are also explicitly called out as a source of spatial uncertainty in heaped-fill stockpiles, which is why survey reconciliation remains necessary even in data-rich environments. Return flows such as fines and pebbles should enter as distinct source terms because downstream comminution is highly sensitive to PSD and because stockpiles themselves can alter feed size distribution through segregation and even in-pile size reduction. Finally, reclaim asymmetry near walls or against concrete structures is not a minor spatial nuisance; both DEM sensitivity studies and live-capacity engineering show that wall effects and feeder placement change reclaim patterns materially. citeturn16view0turn34view1turn11view1turn12search1turn15search1turn3view6

Publicly reported **accuracy thresholds** for coarse ore stockpile composition models remain sparse. The public JKMRC papers emphasize industrial validation of height and product size, but the open-access abstract material does not expose a standardized KPI set for Cu/Mo forecasting. Survey literature is more explicit: one open-pit stockpile study found drone volume error of 2.6% relative to known volume and treated ±3% as the maximum allowable percentage error for volumetric computations, while a 2025 UAS stockpile-measurement study reported volume errors of roughly 4–6% using visual-UAS/Pix4D methods across a broad object-size range, with accuracy strongly influenced by image resolution. The implication is straightforward: geometry and inventory acceptability are often benchmarked in the low-single-digit percent range, while dynamic quality forecasting must usually be commissioned against site-specific baselines rather than a universal industry number. citeturn21view0turn22view0turn5view0

## Required signals and minimum viable instrumentation

The signal architecture below is designed for a decision-grade implementation, not a demonstrator. It prioritizes the signals that govern dynamic stockpile state, reclaim asymmetry, RTD calibration, and quality-to-SAG forecasting. The signal stack is grounded in the stockpile-modeling literature, CA/DEM sensitivity findings, survey/reconciliation practice, and official AVEVA PI integration capabilities. Where the literature does not prescribe an exact scan frequency, the interval shown is an engineering recommendation for a 1–5 minute near-real-time Light Model and an on-demand survey-updated Spatial Model. citeturn18search1turn18search2turn11view1turn3view6turn33view0turn21view0

| Stockpile level / inventory indication | %, m, or estimated t | 1 min | Radar/ultrasonic/laser level tag in PI | Critical | Weakens live/dead stock estimation, level-conditioned RTD switching, and low-level SAG2 starvation logic | Balance-derived virtual level anchored to topographic surveys |

| Assay turnaround / sample timestamp metadata | time stamp, delay | Per sample | LIMS, PI AF, ETL metadata | Medium | Model may be blamed for laboratory latency rather than physics | Automatic sample-delay correction model |

The **minimum viable signal set** for a Light Model that can realistically clear an internal accuracy gate above 80% is: stockpile level or inventory signal, periodic topographic survey, inbound mass flow, at least line-level or grouped outbound flow, feeder on/off states, primary crusher status, feed conveyor status, incoming Cu/Mo by batch or shift, return-flow logging, and one reliable indicator for tractor-assisted reclaim periods. With that stack, you can build a regime-switched mass-balance / RTD model that is operationally useful, even if its spatial fidelity is limited. citeturn24search0turn18search1turn11view1turn16view0

The signals that move the implementation from “useful” toward “decision-grade” — the band you would normally associate with internal confidence approaching 90% — are feeder-level mass flows, feeder openings/speeds, discharge-point direction over the pile, density by ore class, moisture, hardness class, and at least one PSD signal either at reclaim or at SAG feed. Those variables are precisely the ones that the recent stockpile, RTD, and DEM literature identifies as the dominant drivers of segregation, reclaim asymmetry, and downstream comminution response. citeturn3view6turn30view0turn12search1turn26search1

If **individual feeder signals are unavailable**, the light model can still estimate total inventory and plant-average age reasonably well, but it loses the ability to resolve asymmetric draw zones, line-specific lag, and wall-adjacent starvation. In your configuration, that is not a second-order degradation because SAG 2 sits against the wall and already exhibits low-level starvation. In practical terms, missing feeder-level signals forces the model to assume stable reclaim split ratios that the bulk-solids literature explicitly shows are controlled by feeder geometry, spacing, wall effects, and interacting flow channels. citeturn17view1turn15search0turn15search4

## Validation protocol

Because public coarse-ore-stockpile papers rarely publish a universal KPI standard for Cu/Mo forecasting, the protocol below is structured as a **commissioning and governance framework**. The volumetric and RTD elements are anchored to published literature; the production acceptance gates are recommended internal thresholds for a high-throughput copper concentrator digital twin. citeturn21view0turn22view0turn24search0turn37view0

1. **Establish the validation dataset, operating modes, and baselines**

   Use a commissioning dataset with synchronized historian data at 1–5 minute granularity, covering high-, mid-, and low-level stockpile states, crusher stoppages and restarts, feeder outages, return-flow events, and tractor-assisted low-level reclaim windows. A practical starting requirement is 8–12 weeks of history with enough operating diversity to populate each regime; if a pebble campaign occurs only a few times per year, include at least one full campaign before finalizing the reclaim-mode logic. In parallel, define three baselines: a no-lag baseline, a fixed-lag baseline, and a rolling-average quality baseline. Dynamic stockpile literature shows that mean-capacity or fixed-delay representations can be materially wrong under real operating conditions, so the model should only be accepted if it outperforms those baselines consistently. citeturn24search0turn25search0

2. **Use the right KPIs for the right failure modes**

   For **Cu and Mo prediction at SAG feed**, make **tonnage-weighted MAE** the primary KPI because it is the most interpretable commercial error measure and is less sensitive than RMSE to isolated upset periods. Use **RMSE** as the secondary KPI because it penalizes severe misses and is useful during commissioning to surface unstable regimes. Use **MAPE only with caution**: it is acceptable for Cu when the denominator is comfortably away from zero, but it is a poor primary KPI for Mo because small denomators will inflate percentage error. A better percentage measure for Mo is WAPE or a normalized MAE relative to rolling process variability. This recommendation is also consistent with the general RTD and deconvolution literature, which emphasizes mean and variance-based interpretation over ratio metrics when signals are noisy or near zero. citeturn29search3turn37view0

   Recommended internal commissioning gates for **grade forecasting** are: beat the best baseline by at least **20% in MAE** and **20% in RMSE** for both Cu and Mo over the full commissioning window; show no systematic positive or negative bias in any level regime; and maintain a normalized MAE below **25% of the rolling 30-day standard deviation** of the measured grade. A mature production target can then tighten that to **30–35% baseline improvement** and normalized MAE below **20% of rolling variability**. These are recommended governance gates rather than public industry standards, because public coarse-stockpile studies do not yet disclose a universal Cu/Mo benchmark. citeturn5view0turn24search0

   For **granulometry**, use **MAE on P80** and a relative error metric such as **WAPE** or **sMAPE**. Relative metrics are preferable because a 5 mm error means very different things at 25 mm P80 and 80 mm P80. A pragmatic internal gate is **WAPE ≤ 10–12%** during commissioning and **≤ 8%** once the model is stabilized. The reason to keep P80 in the validation pack is that recent stockpile work shows segregation and even in-stockpile size reduction can materially alter SAG feed size distribution. citeturn12search1turn15search1turn22view0

   For **stockpile mass / inventory**, compare model tonnage to survey-derived tonnage at every survey closure and to the online level sensor between surveys. A robust target is **absolute tonnage error ≤ 3%** at each survey closure, with a stretch target of **≤ 2%**. That target is aligned with published stockpile volumetric practice, where ±3% is frequently treated as the outer acceptable envelope for field volumetrics. Between surveys, use normalized RMSE on the online level signal and investigate any drift that cannot be explained by density changes. citeturn21view0turn22view0

   For **RTD quality**, do not rely on a single scalar. Use a bundle of indicators: median lag error for identifiable events, peak-lag error, event-window correlation between predicted and measured downstream response, and the fraction of variance explained by convolving the estimated RTD with upstream signals. A strong internal gate is: **event-window correlation ≥ 0.6** at commissioning and **≥ 0.7** in mature use, with median lag error below **10% of the fitted mean residence time**. These are fit-for-purpose operational targets for dynamic tracing, not a published universal standard. citeturn30view0turn37view0turn39search18

3. **Validate RTD and batch tracking without physical tracers**

   In the absence of RFID or injected chemical tracers, the correct approach is to treat the stockpile as a dynamic system and exploit **natural operational disturbances as quasi-tracers**. Suitable events include crusher stoppages and restarts, abrupt step changes in Cu/Mo due to ore-source changes, hardness shifts, feeder outages, pebble return campaigns, and the specific low-level SAG 2 starvation windows you already observe. RTD theory and generalized n-CSTR modeling are built around the idea that upstream disturbances propagate through the system according to the impulse response; that same logic is directly applicable here. citeturn30view0turn39search18turn39search4

   Methodologically, perform **input-output deconvolution** or **nonnegative impulse-response estimation** on synchronized historical signals. This is an established RTD identification route: recent work shows RTD can be identified from input/output data via deconvolution, and the mining literature on industrial ball mills shows both parametric and non-parametric deconvolution can recover physically consistent RTDs from inlet/outlet measurements. For your use case, the operationally strongest implementation is a constrained, nonnegative finite impulse response or shifted-gamma kernel estimation, followed by comparison against the parametric RTD used in the Light Model. citeturn38search3turn9search0turn37view0

   The practical validation workflow should be as follows. For each natural-tracer event, define an upstream signal window and a downstream SAG response window; prewhiten or detrend both; estimate the dominant lag via cross-correlation; fit the candidate RTD kernel; convolve the upstream signal through that kernel; and then compare predicted and observed downstream response shape, onset time, and tail. When the model is correct, the residual should lose most of its lag structure and systematic bias. When it is wrong, the bias will usually localize to a specific mode: low level, tractor active, pebbles active, or a changed stacking pattern. citeturn37view0turn38search3turn24search0

   In parallel, validate the **mass-balance closure**. At minimum, close total solids balance over rolling 24-hour, 7-day, and survey-to-survey windows. Metallurgical accounting guidance emphasizes that reliable balances depend on accurate mass and metal-content measurement and on accounting periods that are large relative to the process residence time. For an on-line stockpile model, that translates into a governance rule: if mass-balance closure deteriorates before predictive KPIs deteriorate, fix instrumentation and reconciliation first rather than overfitting the RTD. citeturn40search14turn41search18turn41search9

4. **Calibrate the model in a regime-aware way**

   Start with historian cleanup: tag-quality filtering, timestamp alignment, unit harmonization to dry-mass basis, return-flow registration, and interpolation rules for short data dropouts. Then estimate initial inventory from the latest validated survey and back-balance the interval to infer the unmeasured internal state. After that, fit a **baseline gravity-reclaim RTD** using a shifted gamma / tanks-in-series kernel, because that family is compact, interpretable, and widely used in RTD modeling. citeturn18search1turn30view0turn37view0

   Next, split the operating envelope into at least four regimes: **high level gravity reclaim**, **mid level gravity reclaim**, **low level gravity reclaim**, and **tractor-assisted reclaim**. The literature on coarse ore stockpiles, funnel flow, and live capacity makes clear that reclaim behavior changes nonlinearly with geometry and outlet interaction, while the FMS/survey literature makes clear that bulldozers materially reposition material. Therefore, you should not fit one single kernel across all operating conditions. citeturn16view0turn17view1turn34view1

   For the **live/dead stock split**, use a survey-constrained mass-balance and event-response approach. The literature supplies a useful prior but not your site value: simple conical central-offtake systems can have only about 25% live capacity, and large stockpiles frequently struggle to exceed 20–25% live capacity without favorable reclaim design. Your stockpile has eight feeders, so it should outperform a single central offtake, but the low-level SAG 2 starvation indicates that “live” must be estimated by feeder zone, not as a stockpile-wide scalar. The correct implementation is to fit live-stock fraction and RTD parameters by regime, constrained by survey closure and low-level feeder-starvation behavior. citeturn14search14turn16view0turn15search0

   Tractor-active periods should be treated as **contaminated for pure gravity calibration**. Either exclude them from baseline RTD fitting or model them as a distinct reclaim mode with an auxiliary source term that transfers material from conditional/dead sectors into the gravity-live pool. This is the most faithful representation of what the literature calls auxiliary reclaim. citeturn16view0turn11view1turn34view1

   The commissioning gate to move from calibration into production should be conservative: require at least **four consecutive survey closures** or **six to eight weeks** of operation in which the critical KPIs stay within commissioning bounds, with no unresolved systematic bias by stockpile level band, SAG line, or tractor-active mode. That prevents “good average performance” from masking exactly the kind of low-level reclaim failure that matters economically. citeturn21view0turn24search0

5. **Monitor model drift continuously in production**

   KPI computation should be automated **daily**, reviewed by a process/metallurgy owner **weekly**, and formally governed **monthly** and **after every survey update**. That cadence is justified because the model itself is continuous, but the definitive geometric truth enters discretely through survey reconciliation. citeturn11view1turn21view0

   Recalibration should be triggered if any of the following conditions persist: rolling 4-week MAE or RMSE worsens by more than **25%** against the commissioning baseline; survey closure exceeds **3%** for two consecutive surveys; event-lag bias shifts by more than **15% of mean residence time**; or residual bias concentrates in one mode such as low-level operation, SAG 2 only, or tractor-active periods. It should also trigger immediately after material changes that alter the physical stockpile dynamics: feeder modifications, reclaim-tunnel changes, concrete wall changes, crusher CSS changes that alter topsize, belt discharge mode changes, or sustained shifts in ore moisture or fines content. RTD, DEM, and bulk-solids literature all indicate that geometry, charging method, wall effects, and bulk properties are first-order determinants of internal flow behavior. citeturn3view6turn17view1turn12search1

   The production dashboard should be oriented toward operations, not only data science. The most useful views are: real-time inventory versus survey-adjusted inventory; live versus conditional/dead stock estimate; feeder split by SAG line; predicted versus measured Cu/Mo and P80 with confidence bands; event-lag diagnostics; spatial heatmaps of age, Cu, Mo, hardness, and PSD; and a visible tractor-active mode flag. CSIRO’s stockpile management work explicitly emphasizes the value of scenario planning, reclaim optimization, and reconciliation displays, which is directionally consistent with this dashboard design. citeturn11view1

6. **Validate the Spatial Model against topography and downstream outcome**

   At every survey, register the point cloud or DEM to the plant coordinate system and compare the model-predicted surface with the measured surface on a common raster or mesh. Report at minimum: **total volume error**, **z-MAE**, **z-RMSE**, and the **95th percentile of absolute elevation error**. For a production stockpile model used in reconciliation, a practical acceptance gate is **total volume error ≤ 3%** and a stretch target of **≤ 2%**. That is aligned with published field volumetric tolerance in stockpile survey work and gives a credible closure target for the Spatial Model. citeturn21view0turn22view0

   For **sector-level validation**, compare predicted active-sector mass and residence-age distribution to the downstream response after reclaim from that sector begins. In a stockpile, cell attributes are often only partially observable; therefore, geometric validation alone is not sufficient. The operating test is whether reclaim from a sector produces the expected downstream signature in Cu, Mo, hardness, and PSD within the modeled lag envelope. The JKMRC stockpile work is particularly relevant here because its public validation focus is exactly on height variation and product size, not only on static geometry. citeturn5view0turn32search14

   To propagate topographic uncertainty into cell attributes, use **Monte Carlo perturbation** of the survey surface and registration transform, coupled with bulk-density uncertainty bands. Recompute cell masses and tonnage-weighted properties under each perturbation and publish **P10/P50/P90** for total inventory and sectoral attribute estimates. This is the correct governance approach because UAS-based stockpile accuracy depends on survey setup, image resolution, and boundary extraction method; the uncertainty should therefore be carried forward into the digital twin rather than hidden. citeturn22view0turn20search9

## Open questions and limitations

The largest evidence gap is not conceptual but empirical: public literature still provides **very few disclosed plant implementations** of conveyor-fed copper coarse ore stockpiles with **asymmetric reclaim**, **wall-adjacent feeder starvation**, and **tractor-assisted low-level reclaim**. Public papers now show that dynamic CA, RTD, DEM sensitivity, survey reconciliation, and distributed-particle digital twins are all technically feasible, but they do **not** yet publish a shared industry benchmark for Cu/Mo forecast error at coarse stockpile discharge. citeturn5view0turn33view0turn11view3

The second gap is that there is **no universal dead-stock percentage** for your geometry. Open literature supports the qualitative conclusion that large funnel-flow stockpiles can have very low live capacity, often around 20–25% in simple large-stockpile cases, and that feeder geometry can materially improve that. But for an eight-feeder, two-SAG, wall-asymmetric copper stockpile, the dead/live split has to be site-calibrated from survey, feeder, and natural-tracer behavior. citeturn16view0turn14search14turn15search0

The third gap is about return-flow characterization. The literature strongly supports modeling pebbles and fines as separate source terms, but your eventual forecast accuracy will depend on how accurately those streams are timestamped, quantified, and compositionally tagged at the moment they re-enter the stockpile. Without that instrumentation discipline, the model will still function, but uncertainty bands around age, PSD, and hardness forecasts will remain materially wider. citeturn12search1turn24search0
